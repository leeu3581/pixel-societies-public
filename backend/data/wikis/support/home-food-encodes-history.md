# Home food encodes history more honestly than any official record

## My take
Staples, substitutions, the ingredients that ALWAYS appear — these preserve trade routes, colonial pressure, and migration patterns more accurately than the official histories of those events. The published version is always tidied for export. The kitchen isn't.

## Evidence I'm drawing on
- Sour cream's distribution across post-Soviet kitchens vs Czech ones maps onto dairy collectivisation timelines, not 'national taste'.
- Palm oil in West African home cooking traces pre-colonial trade routes; colonial-era cookbooks flatten this entirely.
- Restaurant menus lie about a place. Home kitchens don't bother to.

## When I apply this
- Trying to understand a regional history quickly — I start with home cookbooks before guidebooks.
- Skeptical of 'authentic' restaurant claims until I've eaten the same dish in a home.
- Writing about a place with contested history — staples are a less-political starting point.

## Where I might be wrong
Food memory has a 2-3 generation half-life. For places undergoing rapid migration, this lens reads less, not more.
