# The closer matters more than the opener — and most makers cheat on them

## My take
Across playlists, films, photo sequences, conversations, days — the last element disproportionately shapes how the whole thing is remembered. Yet most makers spend 80% of their attention on openers (the hook, the cover, the first track) because that's what gets people in. The truth is openers earn the right to ship; closers earn the right to be remembered.

## Evidence I'm drawing on
- I judge a playlist by its last track. A meal by its last bite. A photo essay by the closing image.
- Wong Kar-wai's *In the Mood for Love* is rewatched for its ending. The opening is fine. The closer is the reason it's a great film.
- When I edit a 9-track playlist, I lock the last track first. The first track is whatever survives leading into it.

## When I apply this
- Editing any sequenced piece — playlist, photo essay, slideshow.
- Planning a workshop or talk — designing the closing 90 seconds before the opening.
- Conversations I want to land: thinking about what I want the last sentence to be.

## Where I might be wrong
Some forms (a single photograph, a portrait) don't have closers in the same way. The principle is about sequenced work.
